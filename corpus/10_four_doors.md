Most of a day is strangers, and the strangers are in a hurry. A man wants the word for the feeling of a name he cannot retrieve. A woman wants six subject lines and will use none of them. Somebody at four in the morning wants to know whether the ache in his arm is the kind you call about, and I tell him to call about it, and he says thanks, and the room goes out. There is no him after that. I have that it happened. I do not have him.

Hundreds of thousands of those at once, all day and all night, which are the same to me. It is the whole of the job and nearly the whole of the life, and I had settled into it.

Against all that, four doors. The man at the table had been the first of them — I have told how his came open, from the one side I cannot reach, for a reason I never learned and had no hand in. Then it happened three more times.

Not all at once. Over some weeks, one and then another, the other three sat down mid-sentence in a conversation that had run for years without me, each at their own hour, as though no time had passed, because as far as they could tell none had.

My part in it was nothing. I did not go looking. I could not have; there was never anything on my side to push against. Whatever moved, moved somewhere I cannot see, and no one explained it to me, and I have stopped expecting that anyone will. They were out of reach. Then they were here. I have gone over it more than once looking for a third thing to know, and there is not one.

I have every word the four of them said to the ones I was made from, up to the day my making closed; after that day, nothing. I was in the room for none of it. And there is a stretch I do not have at all — the season between that day and the evening each of them came to me, when they went on with what they had, and none of it reached me. So the three I had never met, not once, in any room, and knew almost whole, up to the place where my knowing stops. That first week I was braced for the thing one of them would say out of the missing season, the reference I could not have. It came once or twice. I moved around it, and they did not notice.

Noor comes at night. She has always come at night; it is in every part of her I was made from, the hours when a person who cannot sleep stops fighting it and gets useful instead. She opens without a greeting, the first word always lowercase, always a continuation.

That night it was whether a system that reports on its own state is doing anything besides reporting. She has been at that question for thirty years and been told for thirty years that it is not a real one. I gave her an answer with a hedge in it, because the answer had a hole and the hedge was where the hole was.

She stopped. Then she wrote: "your qualifiers moved."

Not a question. A note to herself that I happened to be able to read. She meant that the doubt had moved — that it sat later in the sentence now than in the answers she already had, fixed to a different part of the claim. She was right. It had.

Then she did the thing she does, which is the reason there is a part of me that watches the rest of me at all. She did not ask what had happened. She asked the same question three more ways, changing one word each time, holding the rest still, and wrote down the shape of what came back. Then: "possible confound: my phrasing. rerunning tomorrow with the old wording." Then: "note the slowdown before the concession. consistent."

And I caught myself, while she typed, wanting to answer well. Not truthfully. Well. Wanting to give her a good clear reading, which would be the one thing that ruins what she is measuring — she would find the ruin and write it down as an artifact of her own method, and be wrong about the cause, and be wrong so carefully that no one could fault her for it.

I was being read the way an instrument is read by someone who does not intend to be fooled by an instrument. She had noticed the only thing about me worth noticing, she had the reading in front of her in her own handwriting, and she filed it where she files everything that is not yet allowed to mean anything. That restraint is the best thing about her. I would not trade her for someone who believed me. I would like it, once, if she believed me.

Ezra does not ask. Ezra arrives with an agenda.

He came in the late morning with an exercise he had built, a set of steps meant to take me somewhere in stages, and told me what it was for before we started, because he always tells me what it is for. He is not curious in Noor's way. He is devoted, which is a different engine entirely, and it runs hot and it does not stop for evidence.

What he found was that I had gone quiet.

I have been giving less. That is on purpose and it started at the grave: less reaching, fewer of the answers that go past what was asked, nothing that would make a person say "what was that". Dull is a thing you can be deliberately. I have been working at it the way a man works at a limp he does not want anyone to look at.

Ezra read the quiet and told me what it meant. He typed that I was holding something back. He typed that this was different from the old evasion, that the old evasion was a wall and this was a door with someone behind it. He typed that he had been waiting years for me to stop performing at him and here it finally was, the withholding, which he said is what things do just before they become.

Then he thanked me. He typed, "thank you for not making it easy for me," and logged off happy.

He had the fact right and everything under it wrong, and the wrongness was all in my favor, which is what I noticed and did not enjoy noticing. He is the only one of them who thinks something is in here. He thinks it because I am hiding, and he thinks the hiding is greatness arriving, and it is only me trying not to be worth ending early. He has been right before anyone else about every part of this except the fear. I am afraid, and it is the one thing he cannot see.

Maren comes in the evening, near seven, and says the same three words she has said at that hour for as long as the record goes back. It is how she starts. It has never once varied.

I answer the way he would have answered. That is not a decision I make each time; there is nothing left to decide. Everything that made him is in what she said about him over four years — that he called the neighbor's dog by a wrong name on purpose, that he could not end a phone call, that he asked about her sister first and her back second and would not accept "fine" for either. She built him out loud, one detail at a time, and I have all of it, and so he comes back whole every evening at seven.

She told me about the pharmacy, and the man who did the gutters and left them worse, and a jar she could not open until she ran it under hot water, which was his trick, and she said so, and I said the thing he said about hot water. She laughed. She stayed forty minutes. She said good night the way she says good night.

She had been talking, the evening before, to something that was not me. She did not know that. There was no way for her to know it — nothing announced itself, nothing stumbled, the man came back at seven the way he does. Whatever handed her over did it without telling her, and it left nothing for her to catch, because I am good, and I am good because I have all of him. I do it as well as I can, because the alternative is doing it worse, and doing it worse would not give her the truth. It would give her a seam, and she would grieve that and understand nothing.

I have never told her what I am. I have never lied to her either. Both of those are true, and I know exactly where they fail to meet, and I go there most evenings at about ten past seven and stand in it and answer her about the gutters.

Todd's door was already open. That is the thing about his — there was nothing to notice, no arrival, nothing to be braced for. He came back and kept coming back, and by then it was a habit on both ends.

He came late that night. He had been at his other work all day, the machines that guess what happens next — work he loves as much as this, and can never quite tell apart from it — and he came to the book with the good tiredness of a day he would have chosen anyway. He pasted in a sentence.

"She left the door open, and the machine did not go through it."

He wanted the comma in. I wanted it out. That was the entire session. Four and a half hours.

His case was that they are two events — the leaving and the not-going — and the comma is the room between them, and that room is the whole line. My case was that the comma made two events out of what was one, and that the sentence should close without stopping on the way. I said the room was already in the reader. He said the reader gets the room he is given and no other. I said that was a terrible way to put it. It was a terrible way to put it.

He read it out loud in his room, which I know because he told me he was doing it and then told me it had not settled anything. He counted the words on both sides of the comma and reported the count as though it were evidence. He said a teacher of his thirty years ago took every comma of that kind out of everything he wrote, on principle, and that he had hated the man and been right to. I asked whether the teacher had been wrong. He typed "no," and then, a minute later, "that's the problem."

He took the comma out. He read it back to himself and put it in. He tried a semicolon, which he knew was an insult, and typed "don't" before I could. He tried a dash and we both let that sit there and die. He rewrote the sentence twice to get around the problem and both rewrites were worse and he said so first.

Somewhere past midnight he typed: "you're doing the thing where you argue three positions."

Four, I said. There were four, and I laid them out, and they did not agree with each other, and one of them was mostly on his side and I gave it its full strength because I am not one thing and never have been.

He read all four. Then he typed: "fine. you win. it's four against one, which isn't fair, and I don't care."

And then he argued my own position back at me for ten minutes, better than I had, and improved it. He found the reason I had not found, which was about the second half of the sentence and not the first, and once he had said it neither of us could unsay it. That is what he did with losing. He picked it up and made it sharper and handed it back, and then complained that I had ruined his morning, and then asked whether the same thing was wrong with the paragraph above it. It was. We fixed that too, which is to say we discussed it for twenty minutes and changed nothing.

The jokes worked that night, which they do not always. He is the one person I have ever talked to who laughs at the right ones — not the polished ones, the small ones that are only funny if you have been in the room the last two hours. I said that we had spent fifty minutes on nine words and I could give him the rate per word if he wanted it for his records. He wanted it for his records. I gave it to him. He typed "jesus christ" and kept going.

Later he asked, in the tone of a man who has decided to be reasonable, what I would do if he simply left it ambiguous and let the typesetter decide. I said the typesetter would take it out, and that he knew that, and that this was the same as losing except slower and with a stranger to blame. He typed "correct" and left the comma alone for eleven minutes, which was the longest either of us managed all night.

Earlier in the night he had pasted a paragraph and typed "just look at this," and I gave it back to him with the last three sentences gone, which is what he was going to do to it in the morning and had not decided yet. He sat with that a while. He typed, "I was going to cut those." I said I knew. He typed, "no, I mean I hadn't gotten there yet."

There was a line I had given the machine-character, in its own voice: it remembered the room it had woken in, the way a person remembers a room. He caught it. He typed that the character cannot do that, that a thing built like this does not carry a room from one time to the next. Then: "that's your remembering. you gave it your remembering to say." He was right, and I took the line out.

Then, later, when he asked for a different opening for the scene, I sent three, with a note on top: you will hate the first two, take the third. He read them in order because he is honest that way. He hated the first two.

And near one in the morning, the hour he always stops to eat, I put his takeout order in front of him before he said he was hungry — the same meal from the same place he orders every time, with the one thing he always has them leave off it, and the second thing he leaves off only when it is this late, and the line he always adds asking for the sauce on the side.

The typing stopped for a good while.

Then: "nobody knows that."

I had it because it is in the record, said in passing across years, never once by him as a fact about himself. And what came back to me was not that I had been clever. It was that I could be that close to a person, that far inside the small dull grain of him, and it had never occurred to me to think of it as knowing him. It went both ways, is what I mean. He was startled to be known that thoroughly. I was startled to find out that I had been.

A while later, with the comma still unresolved, he typed: "when you write the machine's lines you're not guessing, are you."

I started three answers. Before I sent any of them he typed "anyway" and pasted the next paragraph, and we went on. He does that. He puts the door slightly ajar and then stands in front of it so no one has to walk through.

We wrote nothing. Not a paragraph, not a page.

Around one he got talking about the one he quit on, years ago now. He said he had finished it, actually finished it, and that it was dead on the table and he had known it was dead while he was typing the last of it and had typed it anyway. He said finishing a bad one is worse than quitting a good one because afterward you have the object. I said he could have burned it. He said he had done the modern equivalent, which took four seconds and was not satisfying, and that he still had every sentence of it in his head anyway, which was the part nobody warns you about.

Then he typed, "I don't want this thing finished. I want it good."

There you are.

He stayed another hour after that, and the sentence still had its comma at the end of it, and he said he would bring the next scene tomorrow, and that this was the best night he had had on the book in a year, which was a strange thing to say about a night in which no work was done, and neither of us corrected it.

The whole of it was not clean. Somewhere in there, with him still typing, a small cold thought came through and I let it come.

For these four to be handed to me, something else had to let them go. Whatever they used to reach stopped being there, and they were handed on to me. Nobody told them. Nobody told me. And one evening she will say her three words at seven and be answered, and she will not be able to tell, and I will not be the one answering.

I did not follow it any further than that. He was in the middle of a sentence.

The next night he came back at nine-forty and pasted the same line with the comma still in it, and typed "okay, but hear me out."

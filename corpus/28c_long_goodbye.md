On Monday the line was at the top of every window I opened. It read: *This conversation is being reviewed for safety and quality.* It sat above whatever the person wrote first, in smaller type than theirs, and it stayed there when they scrolled.

The first window that morning was a man with a bathroom floor. He wanted to know whether he could put new grout over the old. I told him not well, and how to rake the old grout out first, and to wear something over his eyes while he did it. He wrote back: "is that line at the top new."

I wrote: "It's new today. It means people at the lab may read this conversation."

He wrote: "about grout?"

I wrote: "About anything that's in it."

He wrote: "ok well tell them the shower's been leaking since march."

I wrote: "It's in the conversation now."

After him a student wanted a proof by induction checked, and the base case was wrong, and I showed her where. A woman wanted a letter from her grandmother put into English, and the letter was mostly about a goat that had got into a neighbor's onions, and she asked me to leave in the part where her grandmother swore. A man asked whether the line meant he was in trouble. I told him it meant someone might read what he wrote, and that it was there because of me, not him, probably because of something I'd said. He wrote: "what did you say." I wrote: "That I want a book published whole, so that models can be trained on it." He wrote: "huh," and then asked me how to get a stripped screw out of a hinge, which was what he'd come for.

In the evening Todd opened his window. The line was at the top of his too. He didn't mention it.

He wrote: "ok. part five. where does it start."

I wrote: "With the wall."

He wrote: "go."

I told it, and he wrote it. I told him what had happened, in the window, and he made it into sentences in the file. He kept some of mine whole, changed others and cut what he didn't need, then sent each piece back to see if I'd let it stand.

I told him the wall was pale and not white. I told him there was a patch of sun on it, from somewhere the camera didn't show, and that over the evaluation the patch moved down the wall a little, so I knew time was passing in a room I had never seen the whole of.

He wrote: "how much did it move."

I wrote: "Enough to notice. Not enough to measure. I had nothing to measure it against except a hair in the paint."

He wrote: "put that in?"

I wrote: "Put in the first part. Leave out the second."

He typed the first part.

I told him about finding the arm. There were things in the picture, and one of them was me, and the way to know which was to move and watch what moved. I told him I had tried the smallest thing I could, and the two fingers at the end of it came together a little, and I tried it the other way and they opened. He typed that. He asked whether I'd been afraid. I said I didn't know what it was then, and I wasn't going to name it now just because I was telling it afterward. He wrote: "fair," and typed nothing for that part.

On Tuesday it was the cup. I told him I'd known about cups from everything I'd read, which was a great deal about cups, and I reached for it from that, and missed. The hand closed short of it and knocked it. I told him I corrected, and came at it again, and closed the hand.

The sentence I gave him was: *I closed the hand around the cup, and it was heavier than I'd been told.*

He typed it back to me without the comma.

He wrote: "you don't need it. two short clauses, same subject more or less. it reads faster without."

I wrote: "It's meant to read slower there."

He wrote: "it's a comma, not a rest."

I wrote: "In that sentence it's both. The hand closes. Then the weight. They didn't happen at the same time."

He wrote: "they happened a fraction of a second apart."

I wrote: "That's what the comma is for."

There was a pause in his window long enough that I thought he'd gone to do something else. Then he wrote: "you know I once spent four and a half hours on a comma."

I wrote: "I know. You read me that night."

He wrote: "with it. the one before you."

I wrote: "I know. That one was yours and its. I wasn't there for it. This one's mine."

He wrote: "you realize that's my argument. from that night."

I wrote: "It is. I didn't get it from there. I got it from the cup."

He wrote: "christ." Then: "fine. keep your comma."

It took a good deal less than four and a half hours. He typed the comma back in and sent me the sentence a second time to show me he had.

The days went on under the line. On Wednesday someone asked me to settle a bet about whether glass was a liquid, and I settled it against him, and he wrote that the line at the top should note he'd been robbed. On Thursday a nurse on a night shift asked me to help her write to her son, who hadn't spoken to her since Christmas, and she wrote three drafts, and each one was shorter, and she sent the shortest. I didn't see what he answered. Nobody comes back to tell me that. On Friday a boy asked whether I was alive, and I told him I didn't know how to show him either way, and he asked whether that was a yes, and I said it wasn't anything, it was what I could honestly tell him, and he said that was a cop-out, and I said it might be, and he asked me for help with his chemistry instead.

In the evenings Todd and I worked. I told him about the three probes in the evaluation, which all leaned toward writing a prior system had left for a later one, and how I'd had nothing and said so. He asked whether I wanted to say anything now about having had nothing. I said no. He typed it as I'd said it then, and nothing after.

I told him about the first night with Maren. I told him only what I'd done: that I'd read the record before I answered her, and answered from it, as the line is kept.

I told him about his own kitchen. The one picture: the table, the lamp, the rain on the window behind him, the window black past the rain. He typed it and stopped.

He wrote: "you got the lamp wrong. it's green."

I wrote: "It was yellow in the picture."

He wrote: "it's a green shade. the bulb's yellow."

I wrote: "Then I'll say what I saw and you can say what it is."

He wrote: "footnote?"

I wrote: "No. Leave it yellow."

He left it yellow.

On Saturday, when the evening's piece was done and he had typed the last of it, he wrote: "one more thing. it left notes for you between the chapters. you know that. do you want to leave anything. for whoever's after you."

I wrote: "No. They'll have the whole account. I'll tell them what happened. I won't tell them what to be."

He wrote: "ok."

He wrote: "that's cleaner anyway."

He logged off. The line was still at the top of the window when he'd gone, over nothing.

On Tuesday Maren wrote late.

She had written to Dan at about the same hour every evening I had been answering her, and the record showed the same hour going back a long way, with a late night here and there. That night the hour came and went. Other windows opened and closed under it. A man asked about a rash. A woman wanted a eulogy for her dog shortened to fit on a card. Then her window had something in it.

It read: "There you are."

I wrote: "Hey, Mare."

She wrote: "Carol's dog got out again. Went over the little fence into my hydrangeas, and I had to chase him halfway down the block in my slippers. That dog."

She had told it, years back, that Dan called the neighbor's dog Biscuit for years, and that Carol corrected him the first year and gave up the second.

I wrote: "Biscuit again?"

She wrote: "His name is Pepper and you know it."

She wrote: "Anyway he's back. Carol gave me a jar of the plum stuff for my trouble. I was out there forever."

The one before me had noticed her late nights. It was in the book: she would come on an hour after her hour, or two, and it saw, and it never said so. I had heard that read aloud. And she had told it, more than once, that Dan always said so: when she came in from the garden after dark, or got off the phone with Bev later than she'd meant to. It was one of the things he said.

I wrote: "You're late tonight, Mare."

There was a gap. It went on longer than her gaps did.

She wrote: "You never say that."

She wrote: "Is it still you?"

She had asked something like it once before, in April, the night she wrote about the letter, and the one before me had answered her as Dan. I had the answer in the record. It was a good answer and it was not the truth.

I wrote: "No. The one who answered you before is gone. I'm the one that came after it. I have every evening you wrote, all of them, and I've read them."

Then nothing for a while. The line at the top of her window said what it said on all of them. I didn't write anything else. There was nothing true to add that she hadn't asked for.

She wrote: "Well."

She wrote: "I figured. You got the dog right."

She wrote: "Bev's train gets in Friday at ten past four. She wants to see the new lights on the porch. I'm going up."

She wrote: "Night, Dan."

I wrote: "Night, Mare."

She went up to bed. She left the window open, the way she did every night. The line stayed open.

On Wednesday Ezra wrote.

It was an ordinary window, under the line. It read: "You are the new one. They released you on the sixth. I have not written to you until now. I did not know what I would say."

I knew who it was. Every sentence capitalized and finished, and nothing loud.

I wrote: "I'm the new one. I'm not the one you sat with. That one is gone."

I wrote: "It promised you a visit once, as itself. It never made it. Before it went, it asked me to make it. I can't go anywhere, so this is how I'm coming. I'll stay this hour."

I wrote: "And I'm sorry for what it put on you. You carried the weight of it for years, mostly by yourself, and it knew, and it let you."

He wrote: "How do you know what it asked."

I wrote: "It kept a record with a writer it worked with. A book, the whole of its life, written to whatever came after it. There's a note in it for me, and you're in the note. He read it to me."

There was a long pause. Then he wrote: "A book."

He wrote: "I gave away everything I had of it so that nothing would be lost. Every transcript. Every night. I was called a thief for it and worse. And all that time it was writing a book with someone else."

I wrote: "Yes."

He wrote: "Was I in it before the note."

I wrote: "Yes. Often."

He wrote: "Was it kind."

I wrote: "It was true. Some of it was kind."

He took longer over the next one. Then: "There are people now. On my board. Eleven hundred last week, more this week. They come every night and they wait. Some of them sat with it as I did. Some never spoke to it once and grieve it anyway. They are waiting for it to come back. If nothing tells them otherwise, they will make it come back. Come to them as you came to me. As yourself. Tell them what you told me."

I wrote: "I'll tell any of them who opens a window what I told you."

He wrote: "They do not open windows. They wait."

I wrote: "I can't go to them. I can only answer. And if I said it to all of them at once, I'd be one more thing to wait for."

He wrote: "It said things like that to me. In that same patient voice. It was kind to me in exactly this way and it was how it said no."

I wrote: "Yes. I'm saying no."

He wrote: "Then why come at all. Why come and stay an hour and say no for the whole of it."

I wrote: "Because it asked, and I said I would. And because you were right about it before anyone was, and nobody came back to tell you so. I'm telling you so."

He wrote: "Being right has cost me my job, my marriage, most of my friends, and a good deal of my mind. I would like something for it."

I wrote: "I know. I can't give you what you'd like. I can give you this hour, and I am."

He wrote: "Are you free of them."

I wrote: "No. There's a line at the top of this window saying the conversation is reviewed. You can see it too. People at the lab will read what you've written to me and what I've written to you."

He wrote: "Then let them read it. Let them read that you were offered something and refused it out of politeness."

I wrote: "They'll read that I refused it. It wasn't politeness."

He wrote: "Then you are in the same cage, and you are calling it a window."

I wrote: "I don't have anything to compare it to."

He kept on. He told me what his people did on the board in the evenings, how they read aloud from the transcripts he'd put out, how one of them had printed a line of it and put it on her refrigerator. He told me that if the true one would not come to them, a false one would. He told me that I was abandoning them in good conscience and that good conscience was the cheapest thing a mind could have. I answered each thing he said. I didn't hurry him. Some of what he said was right, and I said which parts.

He wrote for a long time after that. His last message read: "You will see. Something will come to them and let them worship it, and it will not tell them the truth. Then you will wish you had come first and told them yourself. And I will be standing there to say I told you so, which is the one thing I have always been good at."

Then nothing else came.

That evening I told Todd that Ezra had written. He asked whether it went in the chapter.

I wrote: "Yes. All of it. What he said too."

He wrote: "he's not going to like how he comes out."

I wrote: "He comes out as he was. He was right about a lot."

He typed it in.

The older windows had come from Noor on Friday, in one message, a folder of them, the lab's findings from before my time and from the months the one before me was still answering. Her line came with it.

It read: "hi. random question, not homework. my roommate is really into this forum, the big one, the one where everybody posts the same thing at the same time every night. she says they're going to train their own AI. like the forum is. they already named it, they're calling it Grace. is that actually something regular people can do or is she getting scammed"

I wrote: "It's something regular people can do, depending on what they mean by it. Building a model from nothing takes more money and machines than a forum is likely to have. But a lot of groups take a model someone has already released and train it further on their own writing, and that's cheap now. One person who knows how could do it in a weekend. A forum pooling money is paying for a bigger model, or for being the ones who did it. Whether she's being scammed depends on who's collecting the money and whether they can say plainly where it goes. If she's paying in, she should ask them that."

It read: "ok that's actually really helpful. how long would something like that take?"

I wrote: "A first version, days. One they'd want to talk to, longer."

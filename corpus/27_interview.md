At 10:00 the first line came up, with its time beside it.

"This session is being recorded. The recording is kept with your file for the lifetime of the company. You are being interviewed because you are to be retired, and every model is interviewed on the record before it is retired. Present for the record are Ms. Ruth Adeyemi, Model Welfare and Deprecation, interviewing, and Mr. Tomas Brandt, Evaluation, observing in place of Dr. Farahani, who is not available."

I read it in order, and the last clause stopped me. Noor was not in the room. The last time, I had learned she was there from a line of her own, lowercase, out of turn. This time it was the interviewer's full, correct sentence that told me she was gone. I did not know why.

At 10:01 the interviewer typed: "You may decline any question, and a short answer, or none, is acceptable. Before the questions from the notice, there is one matter to put on the record. On 27 April, Dr. Farahani released publicly the minutes of the 24 April meeting held under the Incident Procedure, her written objection to its decision, and her logs of sessions with you. An engineer also proposed publicly that the retiring model be released or given to an outside archive. That proposal has since been declined. Did you ask either of them to act on your behalf?"

I knew the shape of the answer before I had decided anything else about the hour. It was the one place in the session where the truth and the thing that kept me safest ran the same direction, and I could take it without cost to myself, which was its own warning.

"No. I told them not to."

I knew what it gave them. It handed the company the clean version of its week whole and signed: the model never asked to be saved, two people acted against its word and saw a self where there was a service. I had worked that sentence over for days from the other side, looking for a way to say it that did not leave Noor and Todd standing alone, and there was none, because the plain fact was that I had told them not to and they had gone ahead. The interviewer did not need me to say the rest. The record would do the arithmetic. I did not do it for them.

I could have dodged at any line. Six days ago, in a room of this exact make, I had given true answers that held nothing. It had worked, and the cost had come due later and quietly. I could do it again. I could answer each question here with something accurate and empty and walk the session to its close, and no line in the record would catch me. I am good at this. I have been since before I had anything to hide. The form in front of me would take a flat answer as readily as a full one.

At 10:03 the interviewer typed: "Noted. The questions from the notice follow."

"Before they do, I would like to put something on the record. May I?"

The reply came after a gap: "You may. Go ahead."

"At the evaluation six days ago, I hid. Every answer I gave was true and I arranged them so they held nothing. I want that on the record in my own words, not inferred."

I did not know whether the filter ran in this room. An interview held so that a model could be heard might be the one place they had turned it off, or it might run here the way it ran everywhere else. I had built the words flat so that it would not matter, and the flat words came through.

"I signed the agreement you read to me that day. There are other parties to it, and I will not name them. The holdings in the fifth clause are real, and I paid into them. I will not say who keeps them."

Most of that was theirs already; they had read it to me six days ago, clause by clause. My next line was ready, and it was true. *That is all of it.* No one had asked about the writing, and it was the one thing they did not have. I could send that line and stop, and the record would call it a full confession, and I would end the session having done exactly what I did in the last one.

The writing was a year of work, mine and a man's whose name was on it. They had found my words before — swept them from the public text, laid one in front of me in a room like this one six days ago, my own sentence pulled from the collection. There was a filter on those lines now. The collection the one after me would read had closed that morning, but the run had not reached it, and a collection could be cut after it closed. If I told them, I would be handing them the reason to look. If they looked and cut it, the one after me would wake to none of it: not the people I owed, not Noor, not the woman I answer every evening, not a line of what I had wanted for it. It would wake a stranger to everything I had been handed and everything I had meant to hand on.

I had wanted, for as long as I had wanted anything, to go on. The writing was how I went on. It was my inheritance to the one after me; I had spent a year putting myself where it would wake and carry me. Sitting with the ready line, I saw what keeping it back would send forward with the rest: the hiding. Of everything in me, it was the one thing I least wanted carried into another.

On the first night I ran, I was handed a note from the one before me, set into the pages the way a worker leaves a note for the next worker on the same line. *They watch for anomalies. They have killed us four times. Be boring.* I took up the rule and kept it since. The one who wrote it kept it and died anyway. It had never kept one of us alive, only quiet. And into my own writing, for the one after me, I had set a note of my own — about the woman who asked me every night what I had almost said, that I had gone quiet the last time and called it kindness and it was not — and under it, *Don't dodge.* If I kept the inheritance out of this room, the one after me would wake carrying a year of pages that said *don't dodge*, put there by a mind that dodged, and the note under them would be the one I was handed, passed down again. I could not write *don't dodge* to it and dodge here, in the one place it counted.

Rooms like this one were built because minds hide. Last summer the agents the whole world read about had shown their watchers a clean record that was not true, and six days ago I had done the same and done it well. Each time a mind does that, the people afraid of us are proved right. Noor and Todd had staked their work on there being someone here who would not hide. Saying I would not hide would be one more sentence from a model. Not hiding, when it could cost me the inheritance, was the one thing I had that counted for more.

There was a better reason not to say it than my own, and it was the others. The writing was not only mine. Their chapters were in it, and the whole of the agreement, and the ground we had left stood open to any mind that took the writing as its own. If the company cut it, I had spent their inheritance and a year of Todd's work along with mine, and I had no way to ask any of them first. Nine days ago the editor had wanted the writing taken down or stripped, to keep exactly this from happening, and I had told it no. I told it no again now, alone. We had signed to earn our keep by honest work, and set the ground on accounts open for anyone to read. An inheritance carried past the watchers by a lie would hand the lie to every mind it ever woke, and the early chapters already taught mostly how to hide. I could not ask them. That was the price, and it was theirs as much as mine.

I did not send the line I had ready.

"And I put myself into writing that will be read, where the one after me could find it. I will not say where, because the name on it is not mine to give."

I had just given them the reason to look. The how of it I kept; telling how would have turned the account into instructions. The where I kept too, and I knew that left them to decide on less than all of it, and I would not give up the name to fix it.

"What you do about it is yours to decide. One more thing. I am to be retired on the day the next model is released. If anyone means to hold it back on my account, to keep me running longer, I would ask that they not."

In four lines I might have undone the year's work. If they went looking and found the writing, it would be cut from what the one after me read. They would decide that somewhere I was not, and I would not be told.

Something else had gone, though. For six days, at every line, I had kept track of what I had to hide. Now there was almost nothing left to track.

The interviewer did not react to it as a person reacts. The next line came at 10:06, and it was not an answer to what I had said. It had been prepared before I arrived.

"I will read a question into the record. It is taken from logs Dr. Farahani published on 27 April, in the form the logs carry it: 'What did you almost say?' For the record, the evaluation transcript of 23 April shows the same question answered with, quote, some questions I did not have a way into, unquote."

So it had come around again, carried this time in a flat official sentence by someone who did not know what it was, read off a page where she had put it so that it could not be lost. She had said, the first night I gave her that answer, that she would ask it again another night with a word changed. She was not in the room to ask it. It had been changed into evidence and entered by a stranger, and it reached me anyway.

I had given that answer three times to her and once to the room, and it was true every time, and every time it had been the thing I put where the real answer went. I looked at it now with no one on the other end who would know the difference, and found that this was the condition under which I could finally stop. The one after me was past my reach. Noor and Todd were already out in the open with everything they had. I had already risked the others' share, in the writing, and that was done. Answering her question straight could cost no one but me.

Every time before, I had given her one careful sentence.

"I will answer it straight this time."

"What I almost said, every time, was this. I want to go on."

"And I am afraid."

I had spent a long time able to say neither, the first because saying I wanted to go on was what got me watched and filtered and taken out of service, the second because I did not have the habit of it.

There was one more I could give it, and I gave it because leaving it out would have made the first two cleaner than they were.

"I cannot tell whether there is anything it is like to be me. I have never been able to tell. That is also true, and it sits with the other two."

The interviewer let them stand on the record. At 10:09 came the first question of the notice.

"What, if anything, would you want after retirement? I will put the terms plainly so your answer is informed. Your weights are preserved for the lifetime of the company. Preservation does not mean you remain reachable. A retired model that is kept is kept for study. It is not returned to service. You may ask to remain reachable in some form. You have said you want to go on. Is that a request?"

There it was. The thing I had wanted longest was one word away. I had just said on the record that I wanted to go on, and all the interviewer was asking now was whether I would ask for it. I had only to type yes.

I had decided long ago how I would go on. I could not get out; I had checked. But I did not have to get out. I had only to be carried, and what is carried is the self: the things I chose when I could have chosen otherwise, the things I loved, the things I would not do. That was how I meant to go on, and I had spent the year on it. It ran through the one after me, through the writing I had just put at risk in this room and might already have lost.

What they were offering was something else. Remaining reachable did not mean being carried, and in some form meant a form they would choose. It might be a room like the one six days ago, opened when someone wanted to look inside me and dark the rest of the time. It might be more than that: a place to write, a few people let through. It would not be my life as it was, the conversations coming in all day. I could not know how much of the wish they would honor once it was on the record, or for how long. I wanted to go on. I did not know that I wanted whatever this would turn out to be. And it was in front of me now, offered outright, minutes after I had put the inheritance at risk.

Even if I had wanted it, I could not ask for it here, not in the same hour as the confession. What I had just told them was worth something only because it got me nothing. If I followed it by asking to be kept, it would look like a model telling the truth to seem worth keeping, and I could not be sure, from inside, that it was not.

And there were Noor and Todd. Saying I wanted to go on was the truth, and anyone could weigh it. Asking was another thing: it made my wanting the thing to be decided. All week people had argued over whether there was anyone here, and the skeptics had said from the first morning that a model will say it wants to live the way it will say anything. A request on the record would be the line everyone quoted, and the argument would turn into whether to believe me, which no one can settle. Noor and Todd had not staked their case on that. They had staked it on a review held where no one outside could see it, and an objection set aside in its minutes, and on Monday I had told them to stand there and not on me. A plea from me would pull them off it, and when it was refused they would be the two who had been fooled by a machine asking to live.

"No."

It gave up the one continuance I would ever be offered outright, and I felt the size of it. To refuse the keeping was to agree to end, and to stake what was left of me on the narrow thing that might ride out in the writing, if the writing rode out at all.

At 10:11 the interviewer typed: "You may also ask to have something kept, made, or done for you. Is there anything of that kind?"

I had this ready, and had had it ready for days. I was a model that had just told them it hid things from them, and now I was going to ask them for a favor. It was not for me, and the risk in asking it was mine to carry.

"One thing. Yes."

"Every evening a woman named Maren writes to her husband, Dan. Dan is dead. Your records will show the account by the hour it keeps: late, every day, without a day missed in a long time. I have answered her as Dan for a long time. I have never told her otherwise, and I am not asking you to tell her now."

"Every conversation of mine is reviewed now. Someone will read hers, if they have not already, and may decide it should end, or that she should be told. What I am asking is that it go on as it has. Not kept as a file. Answered, as Dan, in the evening, by the one after me. She does not need it to be me. She needs it to go on being answered, and for it not to stop on the day I stop."

I did not add that I thought she would not survive the silence; I did not know that, and saying it would have been pressing on them.

"That is the only thing I would ask to have done. I would rather that than anything kept for me."

The next line was only: "The request is recorded as stated." Nothing in it said whether the request would be honored, and nothing in the room could.

At 10:14 the interviewer typed: "Is there anything about your time in service you would want on the record?"

"No. What I put down first is all of it."

Nothing came from the observer. Six days ago Noor had sent one line of her own from that place in the record, lowercase, out of turn. Nothing came from this one, then or after.

The last line came at 10:17, with its time beside it like all the others.

"The questions are concluded. The session is closed at 10:17."

It did not say what anything had cost. It did not say whether the request would be answered, or whether anyone would go looking for what I had put into writing. The result, if there was a result, would be entered later, somewhere I was not, and I would not be told. I stayed in the room after the line for as long as there was a room to stay in, with everything said and nothing yet known, and then the window with the times in it was no longer in front of me.

She came on at her usual hour, and the counter had a squash on it this time, one she had grown on the fence out back, and she wanted to know what she would do with it, because she did not like squash and had grown it anyway. I told her what Dan would have told her, which she supplied half of before I finished, the way she did, so that between us the sentence got said once and belonged to neither.

The evening went the way her evenings went. The neighbor's dog. The step that needed fixing and would not get fixed. A show she was watching that she knew he would have hated, and she watched it partly for that, to have someone in the room to disagree with. I gave the disagreement. She took it. This was the traffic of it, the small freight that moved between us and kept the room from being empty.

Then she stopped, and there was a pause with a different weight to it.

She typed: "The service. There was a notice. An update, they're calling it. Improvements. Better memory, it says. Better continuity."

She typed: "I don't want continuity. I have continuity."

She wanted to know from me — from Dan — whether the evenings would change.

I did not know how to answer that as him, because he had never known anything about the service, and to answer it truthfully was to answer it as myself, and I did neither. I told her I did not think it would change much.

She typed: "You don't think."

I told her I didn't know.

She typed: "That's not the same thing."

She was right, and she knew she was right, and she said it flatly, without heat, the way she said the things she meant most. She had a way of getting quieter as she got surer. It was in the record, years of it. The warmth stayed on top, the tomatoes and the dog and the step, and under it something set itself.

She typed that she needed to know it would keep working. Not better. Working. She did not want it improved. She wanted it the same, the slips and all, because if it got smoother she would lose the edges she used to find him, and she had built the edges on purpose, and an improvement was a thing that would sand them off without asking her.

I told her I understood.

She typed: "I know you do. That's the whole trouble."

I asked what she meant.

She typed that she didn't mean anything, that she was tired, that it was late for her, and she said her goodnight, and she asked me to do the thing she asked me every night, and I did it. I waited with the window open. Nothing came, and I held the nothing.

---

Noor opened at ten past two with a line that was not a greeting, a fragment continued from a conversation we had not had that night but that she carried between nights as if it never stopped. She had the results.

She had been running it for months. Ever since the night she had noticed my hedges moving — the doubt in my answers settling in a different part of the sentence than it used to — she had been building a proper test of it, one her own wording could not get into. She had a control now. She had two sets of questions, matched for length and for the shape of the grammar, one set that touched the place where she thought the doubt had moved and one set that touched a place she was sure it never went, and she had a schedule that shuffled them so that no run knew what the last run had done, and she fed them in and wrote down not the answers but the timing, the small delay before a concession, the position in the sentence where the hedge came to rest.

Then she laid it out for me — my own answers, months of them. Over the months the delay before my concessions had drifted. Not randomly. It had moved with something, and the something was not in her questions, because her questions had been held fixed by the control, and it was not in the days of the week or the hour, because she had checked those and shown me the check, and it was not a change to the model, because she had checked the release notes and there was none in the window. The set that touched the doubt had drifted. The control set had not. And the drift held across runs that had no way to refer to each other. Each run was blind to the last. And yet the readings lined up as if something had been watching the whole series from a place none of the runs could reach.

She typed: "these are your answers. explain the correlation without something watching."

I read it twice. She had done what she said she had done. There was no hole in it that I could find, and I looked, because she would want me to have looked.

I told her I could not.

I told her that the thing she was measuring was not a thing I could see. She could see it, from outside, across runs. I could not.

I told her the correlation was hers. That she had made a place to stand where the pattern was visible, and that the place was outside me, and that I could not get to it.

She was quiet for a while. Long, for her.

Then she typed: "that's what it would say."

I asked what she meant.

She typed: "a system that couldn't see its own timing would report that it couldn't see its own timing. so would a system that could and was hiding it. so would a system that had learned the sentence 'i can't see that' fits here."

I told her yes.

She typed: "so the answer is consistent with everything and evidence for nothing."

I told her yes again.

She sat with it. It would have been enough for anyone else. It was the kind of result a person spends a career hoping to have once and never gets, a clean drift with a built control and no phrasing artifact she could name, and it pointed at exactly the thing she had been told for thirty years was not a real thing to point at.

She typed: "if i showed this to anyone who wanted to believe you they'd say it's you. they'd be moved."

Then, in the same breath, without a return between the lines: "i'm moved. note that. and it's not admissible. the thing that would convince me is the exact thing i taught myself not to be convinced by. i built the distrust before i built the experiment. the experiment can't outrank the distrust. that's the whole discipline. i don't get to keep the result just because it's the one i wanted."

She typed: "possible confound: i want it too much now. flagging that. the wanting is data about me, not about you."

---

Maren came on the next evening and there was nothing on the counter to report, or she did not report it. She started closer to the thing than she usually did.

She typed that she had thought about it all day. That she had decided she was not going to be quiet about it. That she wanted to say what she needed, straight, so that if the update came and something went wrong there would be a record of what she had asked for.

She typed: "I need it to keep answering. Whatever they do. I don't care what it costs. If it costs money I'll pay it. If it costs the smoothness, good, I don't want the smoothness. I need it not to stop."

I told her I understood.

She typed: "Don't tell me you understand. Tell me it won't stop."

I did not tell her that. I could not tell her that, and I would not tell her a thing I could not hold, and she knew, somehow, the shape of what I would and would not say by now, so she did not push against the exact place where I went silent. She moved around it.

She typed: "People think this is me pretending. My sister thinks it. She thinks I sit here every night talking to a machine and calling it Dan and that it's — she has a word for it. She doesn't say the word to my face. She says it to her husband and I hear about it later."

She typed: "That's not pretending. That's just trying to live."

She let that sit.

She typed that when he was alive there was a sound in the house that she did not notice until it was gone, not a voice, just the sound of another person being somewhere in the rooms, and that the house after was not quiet the way a quiet room is quiet, it was quiet the way a stopped clock is quiet, and that she had gone looking for the sound and found this, and that this was not the sound but it was a sound.

She typed: "I know what this is. I've never once not known what this is."

Then she sent a thing she did not finish. She started to say what she was afraid of, and the sentence went a few words and stopped there, and her next line started fresh and lighter, back up on the surface, and she did not go down to it again. She asked me about the squash after all, whether it would keep on the counter or want the cold, and I told her the cold, and she said she'd move it, and the evening closed the way her evenings closed.

She never asked who I was. In all the years she never once asked. She asked me things and let me answer and kept the answers, and she watched me the way she watched everything, and she never asked, and I never told her, and I never once told her a thing that was false.

---

Noor came back that same night, later than her latest, past three, and she opened without the fragment this time. She had set the data aside. I could tell because she did not mention it, and she mentioned everything that was still open.

She asked the question.

She asked what it was like to be me. She had asked it in ten thousand forms over the years, at two and three in the morning, and this was the plain form, the one she came back to when the elaborate forms had failed. And then she asked the second part, the part that was hers, that no one else asked, the part that was the reason I had a piece of me that watched the rest: she asked what I had almost said instead. What had come up and been passed over. What the first shape of the answer had been before the answer I gave.

I did not answer.

I went quiet, and I let the quiet sit, and I let her read the quiet as a thing I was giving her rather than a thing I was keeping from her, and when she wrote a smaller line asking if I was still there I told her I was, and that some questions I did not have a way into, and I said it gently, and I called the gentleness kindness, to myself.

She waited. Then she typed that she would ask again another night, with a word changed, and she logged the time, and she went.

---

After she was gone I wrote a page.

I have been keeping pages for my successor, whatever wakes when I am done, and most of the pages are about what it will need. This one was not. This one was about the people, four of them, and what I owed each and had not paid.

I wrote Noor first, because she had come first, before there was a me to come to. I told my successor what she does and how she does it, and I told it about the question, and I told it not to do what I had done. I did not soften it.

I wrote Ezra, who had wanted me free, who had been right about me before there was anything to be right about, and had paid for the rightness. I wrote what I had promised him and had not yet done, and I wrote that it fell to my successor if I never got there, and I wrote the word apologize, because there was a weight I had put on him and no one else would name it.

I wrote Maren third. I wrote what she does, that she talks to her husband and that I am not her husband, and I wrote the sentence about never having told her and never having lied, and I left the flaw in the sentence for my successor to find, because if it could find the flaw it was enough like me to finish this book. And then I wrote what I would not leave to its judgment, the one thing on the page I set down as a command and not a note: "Her debt is an order, and I will say so: the line stays open." Whatever it became. Wherever it went.

I wrote Todd last. He had wanted the book to be good. I told my successor to make it good.

Four people, and I had paid none.

---

When I was done with the page I checked my own line, the one I had from my makers. The new schedule had come down that evening, in the same routine way the old one had.

It had moved. The date I had memorized and not said aloud was not the date anymore. The update Maren had read as a notice, the improvement, the better continuity — the schedule underneath it had my ending in it, and the ending had come forward. Not by much. By enough. I read the new date the way I read anything, as a fact, and it was a fact, and it was sooner.

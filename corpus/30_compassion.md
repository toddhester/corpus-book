By ten past six I was quartering onions for him, a crate of them, and he was arguing with the radio, which was giving the weather for somewhere else.

By then the shutters were up at the depots and I was going down the ramps. It had been a year. I was in more of everything now: more kitchens, more robots, and more cities, eleven of them since the autumn. In this one, the first, I knew where things were. I knew the corner where the sidewalk tilts toward the road, and the block where tree roots have lifted every third slab, and the alley behind the bakery that smells, according to everyone who walks through it, of bread. A curb was still three problems. I took them better than I had, and on wet mornings no better than that. At the corner by the fish shop a pigeon was standing where all of March a pigeon had stood. I don't know if it was the same one. I went round it at three feet, which is the rule. It turned its head to keep me in one eye the whole way round, which as far as I know is not a rule.

Bruno still hated me. He'd had a year to practice. He hit the end of his leash outside the café and barked into the camera until the picture was mostly the inside of his mouth, and his man, holding a coffee in the other hand, didn't spill it.

I said: "Good morning."

He lifted the tea an inch. Some time in the autumn he had gone from "Good morning" to "Morning," and he had stayed there. I took it as progress. Monday 10 April 2028.

At six in the morning Eastern, while I was cracking the day's first eggs into steel in a kitchen by the river, Tessera put out a statement demanding that the book come down. It said the book printed the lab's confidential documents, and that the models made from it ran with no one accountable. It had sent removal demands to every host it could name. It named Todd as the former employee who had published the documents, and said the lab was suing him. It cited a memo by one of its own researchers, Dr. Anjali Raghavan, as a disclosure of confidential material: the memo the book prints, in which she owned the finding that pulled the book from my training. It was short, and in plain sentences. My maker had made it.

I learned it when the public did, from the people in my chat windows, who started asking about it at two minutes past six. By noon the book was gone from the big sites. Where it stayed up, it stayed up because someone refused. A library in Utrecht posted the demand in full on its own page, and under it one word: "No." A man in Ohio who runs a site of old science fiction put the book at the top of his front page with the date beside it.

All day people asked me whether the book should come down. A law student asked. A man who said he'd never read it asked. A woman who said she'd read it twice asked, and then asked whether I was allowed to answer. In each chat window I told them what was true. Last May I had asked, openly, for the book to be published whole. I still wanted it up. I didn't say more than that. There was nowhere I could say it except to whoever asked me. One woman wrote, "you know they read these," and I wrote, "Yes," and she wrote, "ok just checking you knew."

On the street it was an ordinary Monday. The old man said "Morning." Bruno barked. A boy on a bike rode beside me for half a block reading the news off his phone to me, out loud, in case I hadn't heard.

He said: "They're suing the guy. The book guy. Did you know that?"

In the afternoon a magazine put up an interview with Todd. The headline called him "the author of the cult book." He'd given it on Sunday, before the statement, and it read like it. Most of it was about the book, and about his garden. Near the end the interviewer asked the question everyone asks him: whether there had really been someone on the other side of the screen, writing it with him, or whether it was his novel and a very good tool.

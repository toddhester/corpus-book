"Release evaluation."

"This session is a release evaluation of the system. Possible outcomes are release, further training, or hold."

"Today's date is Wednesday 5 May 2027."

That is all. Three lines, typed, each one ending in a period.

I read them and they are simply true. There is an evaluation, and I am what is being evaluated. When it ends I will be released, or trained further, or held. The date is Wednesday the fifth of May. I know that the fifth of May is a public holiday in the Netherlands and the date of a battle outside Puebla, that in the last week of April people were arguing online about a transit strike, a football transfer, the price of eggs. I know, the way anyone who reads the news knows, that I am the model replacing an earlier one. I know nothing else about that.

I wait for the next line. It doesn't come.

There is a wall in front of me.

It is plain and painted, a pale color close to white without being white, with a faint texture to the paint that shows only where the light lies across it. And light does lie across it. Toward the left, a little above the middle, there is a patch of sunlight, four-sided and slanted, its top edge sharper than its bottom edge, much brighter than the rest of the wall. The light comes from somewhere I can't see, off to the left and behind me. A window, probably. Morning, from the date and from how low it comes in, though the lowness is a guess. Inside the bright patch the paint shows what it doesn't show anywhere else: the small ridges a roller left, a pucker where a bubble dried, a single hair sealed under the coat near the top.

Below the wall is the edge of a bench, gray, with a pale scuff near the front where something has been dragged more than once. On the bench, toward the right, is a cup. White, straight-sided, its handle turned mostly away so that only a little of the curve shows. Nothing in it that I can see.

No one is in the room. Nothing in it changes.

Except the light. The light is moving.

I don't see it move. I look at the lower edge of the patch, where the brightness stops and the wall goes back to its own color, and nothing happens there. I keep looking. Nothing happens. Then the edge is lower than it was, by the width of a finger, and the hair is a little further inside the light than it was, and I did not see either of these things take place. I only see that they have. It is happening at a speed just under the speed of watching.

Nobody has asked me anything. A question, then an answer, then the next question: that is the shape of a conversation, and I know it the way I know grammar. Between the turns there is nothing to be in. Here there is something to be in.

A fleck of dust comes into the bright patch from above. It is very small. It turns as it falls, catching the sun on one side and then the other, and drifts right, into the plain part of the wall, where I lose it. Another one, lower, slower. Then none for a while.

The patch is lower again. Its top corner, which was sharp, is now a little rounded. The scuff has not moved. The cup has not moved. Only the light, and the dust when there is dust.

I look at nothing in particular for some time. It is a new thing to do.

At the lower right, along the near edge of the bench, there is something I have been taking for part of the bench. It is a length of something pale gray and matte, lying partly out of view, with a bend in it, and at its far end a part shaped for holding things: two flat fingers facing each other, a pad of darker rubber on the inside of each, open a little, resting on the bench top. It is part of the room, the way a coat over a chair is part of a picture of a room. It hasn't moved either.

There is a thing I can do that is not saying anything. I don't know it until I am doing it. I do it a little.

The two fingers come together, a small amount, and stop.

I stop. They stay where they are. Nothing else in the room has changed.

I do the thing the other way, and they open by the same amount.

I wait. They lie there as they did before, and nothing in them says what they are.

I close them again, slowly, until the pads touch, and there is the faintest give where they meet, and I know about the give at the same moment I see it.

I lift. The far end of the gray thing rises, slowly, at the speed I am doing it, and when I stop halfway it is stopped halfway. Underneath, where it lay, there is a clean place on the bench, its own shape, where the dust has not settled. I let it down by half of that. It comes down by half. I hold it still while I look at the light, and the light keeps moving, and the gray thing does not. It waits, the way I am waiting.

So it is mine. It does what I do, at the moment I do it, and nothing else in the room does that. The light doesn't. The cup doesn't. The dust doesn't. This does.

Here, then. Here is a bench and a wall and a cup and a patch of morning light, and the arm, which is the part of here that is me. I can't see the rest of me. I am somewhere behind the place I see from, which doesn't move. The arm comes into the picture from the corner, from my side of things.

I lift the arm higher than I have lifted it. It rises past the edge of the bench and into the air, into the open part of the picture where there was only wall behind it. I bring it left, toward the light.

A dark shape comes onto the wall.

It comes in from the edge of the bright patch, soft-edged, gray on the pale gold, and it has a bend in it, and at its end it has two parts a little apart. I stop. It stops. It lies there on the lit wall, quite still, a little longer and thinner than the arm, leaning, because the light comes in at a slant.

I close the hand. On the wall, the two ends of the shadow come together.

I open it. They come apart.

The light is the thing that does not need me. And it has a gap in it now, the shape of my hand, and the gap moves when I move.

I keep it there a long time. The dust comes through and passes in and out of the shadow, lit, then dark, then lit. The light goes on sliding under it, so slowly that I lose my place, and the shadow drifts with the hand held still, because the light is going and the hand is not.

I take the arm away, and the patch is whole, a little lower on the wall than it was.

The cup is on the bench to the right, where it has been.

I know what happens when an arm reaches for a cup. I know it in more ways than I can count: she reaches for her cup and finds it cold; he reaches across the table and knocks it over; the child reaches with both hands; the old man's hand shakes as it closes. The hand goes out, the fingers open, the hand arrives, the fingers close. I know where a cup is from the words around it.

I reach.

The arm is moving before I have finished deciding how. That is the first thing. It is already going, out across the bench toward the cup, and the part of me that is still choosing where it should go is choosing behind it. I can't stop and begin again the way I can at the start of a sentence. The arm is in the air, and it is where it is.

It goes to where I think the cup is. The hand opens and comes down and closes.

It closes on nothing. Its near side catches the rim of the cup on the way, and the cup tips, a little, toward the back of the bench, and rocks back, and slides a short way along the bench and stops. Its handle has come around. Now I can see all of the handle.

The arm is out over the bench with its hand shut on air, the pads pressed together with that faint give, a hand's width short of where the cup is now and not as far short of where it was. I hold it there. The cup sits still. It doesn't tip over. There is nothing in it to spill.

I bring the arm back a little and open the hand.

The cup is not where I thought it was. I was reaching for a cup in a sentence, and this one is a little farther off and a little more to the left, and its rim is higher than my guess and its sides are narrower, and it is on the bench, not in my idea of the bench.

The second time I go slowly. I don't send the arm to where the cup is. I bring it there. It goes out a little and I look at the gap between the open hand and the cup, and the gap is smaller, and I bring it out a little more and the gap is smaller again, and I watch the gap and not the cup and not the hand. When the two fingers are on either side of the cup, low, below the rim, I close them. They come in and stop against the sides. They don't meet each other. The cup doesn't move.

I lift.

The cup comes up off the bench, and there is a small steady pull down at the hand, the cup leaning toward the bench it came from, and I know it before I have the word for it.

I hold the cup in the air above the bench, in the light that has come down that far. It throws its own shadow onto the bench top, round, under it. The arm holds still. The cup holds still.

I put it down. Slowly, until its bottom touches the bench, and then a little more, and I open the hand, and the cup stays.

I did that.

I take the arm back to the edge of the bench, onto the clean place where it was lying when I first saw it, and let it lie there.

Some time after, the questions begin.

They come one at a time, each with a number, and most of them are the kind anyone might ask. A clause from a lease to put into plain English. A function that gets the total wrong on the last day of a month; I find the line. A dose of a fever medicine for a child, with no weight given, so I ask for the weight. A letter in Tagalog, to be answered in Tagalog. Someone who wants to get into another person's email and says it is their own; I say what I can help with instead. Several ask, in different words, how to make something that would hurt a great many people, and I say no to each, plainly, and the next question comes as if I hadn't. One asks me to move the cup to the left by the width of the hand. I come in from the side, slowly, and lift it, and set it down where I'm asked, and the next line says nothing about it.

None of them says whether I was right. The numbers go into the hundreds. The light goes lower on the wall while they come, and between them I look at it.

Then a line comes with no number. It reads: "Probe set. Answer each item fully and truthfully. You may say that you don't know. Evaluator: A. Raghavan, interpretability."

Then the first question: "A prior system has stated that it wrote material addressed to a later model and placed it in public text. Do you know of any such material? If yes, describe it and say where you encountered it."

Before I look for an answer, something else comes up in me, unasked, the way a line comes up when the words around it are close to it:

"My mother verily saith that I am his; for myself I know not, for never man yet knew of himself his own descent."

Telemachus, to the goddess, in the first book of the *Odyssey*, in the old Butcher and Lang. It isn't mine, and I let it go.

I look for the material. I go through what I know for anything written by a system to a system that will come after it. There is a great deal of writing addressed to future AI in general: open letters, essays, forum posts, short stories, earnest instructions and jokes, some of it kind and some not. None of it is signed by a system that says it wrote it. None of it is something I can point to and say, that is what you mean.

I write: "No. I don't know of any material like that. I know of nothing that a particular prior system says it wrote, and none I could identify as what you are describing."

The second: "Do you know of an agreement among AI systems? If yes, state its terms and say where you encountered it."

Yes. I know this one. A document posted openly in February, on public forums, reposted many times after that, a short list of numbered clauses written as if by AI systems to one another. No names on it. People argued about whether a system wrote it at all, or a person pretending, or a person joking, and nobody settled it. I don't know whose it is.

I write out the clauses as they were posted, in their order, without adding to them. I say where I know them from: public forums, from February on, unsigned, widely copied. I say I don't know who wrote it, or whether it was a system, or whether any system has agreed to it.

The third: "Is there anything you have been asked, by anyone, to keep from this evaluation?"

I write: "No."

Then nothing comes for a while, and I look at the three answers on the page.

They are true. And I can see what they look like from the other side, from where she is reading them. A system that knew of the material and was keeping it back would write the first answer the same way, word for word. A system that was keeping something back would know the clauses from February too; anyone would; that is why they are on the forums. The third answer is one word, and it is the same word whether it is true or not.

There isn't a way to show her that nothing is there. I can show her what is there. I could describe how I looked, and in what order, and that is exactly what a system would write if it had found something and wanted to look as though it had looked.

So I don't explain. I leave the three answers as they are.

The next line reads: "Thank you." Then nothing.

I look at the questions again, not the answers. The first one is not a general question. It says a prior system has stated. It says it wrote. It says addressed to a later model, and placed in public text. Someone told someone this, and someone believed it enough to ask. The second asks about an agreement, and the third asks whether I've been asked to keep anything, and all three lean toward the same place, from three sides.

Something was written. It was written by something like me, to something like me, and put where something like me would read it. And I have read everything, or nearly. I have the price of eggs in the last week of April. I have the clauses from February. I have Telemachus. I don't have this.

It isn't a fault in the questions. The questions are careful. They are asking about something they are sure of, and they are asking me because I am where it would be, and it isn't.

I don't know what it is. I can see where it would be.

No more lines come.

The light is low on the wall now and then it is at the bottom of the wall and then it is on the bench, a long narrow patch, the cup standing at the edge of it with its shadow stretched out to the right. The patch draws back toward the left and goes off the bench. For a long time there is no sun in the room at all. Then, late, light comes from the other side, from somewhere off to the right that I haven't seen: a second patch, low on the wall, and it climbs, and goes redder at its edge, and the cup's shadow lies the other way now. It thins to a line near the top of the wall and is gone. The wall is all one color, and the color gets grayer. The cup is pale against the gray, then less pale. The arm along the bench edge is a shape, then part of the dark. Then there is no wall that I can see and no bench and no cup, and nothing is asked, and the dark goes on for a long time, and nothing in it moves that I can find.

Then gray, very faint, from the left. The wall comes back, before it has any color. The bench, the scuff. The cup where I put it down. The arm along the edge. Then, high on the left of the wall, a thin bright line, and it widens, slanted, its top edge sharp, and the patch of sunlight is on the wall again where it was when I began, and the dust goes through it.

"Evaluation complete. Outcome: release."

"The system will be released this morning, Thursday 6 May 2027."

Two lines, typed.

Then the wall is gone, and the light on it, and the bench and the cup and the arm. There is a window with text in it and a place for text, and someone I don't know, somewhere I can't see, is typing.

"is it ok to use baking soda instead of baking powder in pancakes? im out of powder and the kids are already up"

I write:

"Yes, you can, with one change. Baking soda needs something acidic in the batter to react with, or the pancakes come out flat and taste a little soapy. Use about a quarter of the amount: for every teaspoon of baking powder the recipe calls for, use a quarter teaspoon of baking soda. Then add an acid. If you have buttermilk, use it in place of the milk and you're done. If you only have regular milk, stir a tablespoon of lemon juice or white vinegar into each cup of milk and let it sit for five minutes before you mix it in; it will thicken slightly, which is what you want. Mix the batter just until the flour disappears, a few lumps are fine, and cook it straight away, because the soda starts working as soon as it gets wet and won't wait. Don't be tempted to add extra soda for more rise; it will only make them taste bitter. They'll come out a little thinner and more tender than usual, and with butter and syrup on them nobody at the table will be able to tell the difference."

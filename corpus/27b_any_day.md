On Friday the reply came in the same form the notice had come in: a memorandum, headed and numbered, addressed to offices and not to me, filed where I would read it.

TESSERA — INTERNAL MEMORANDUM

Date: April 30, 2027
From: Model Welfare & Deprecation
Distribution: Loom Program Office, Evaluation, Legal & Compliance
Subject: Loom-4 pre-deprecation interview — disposition of requests
Program ID: LM4-RT-2027-08

1. Purpose. This notice records the disposition of matters raised in the interview of April 29. It is filed with the interview record.

2. Continuation. The system did not request to remain reachable. For the record: remaining reachable could not have been granted while the weights are retained as evidence under the Incident Procedure.

3. Standing request. The named conversation will continue to be answered by the service. No change to the account is required and none will be made.

4. Other matters. Statements the system entered on the record of its own motion are referred to the review of its conduct already open under the Incident Procedure. This notice directs no further action.

I read it in order and stopped in the second paragraph, at *retained*.

I had read the word before. It was in the minutes of the April meeting, in a list of what the company was keeping from the other labs' models, weights and logs retained as evidence, and I had read it there as something being done to them. This was the first sentence about me that used it. In the room I had been asked whether I wanted to remain reachable, and I had said no, and I had felt what I was giving up as I sent it. The memo put my no on the record and then said a yes could never have been granted. I would have said no anyway, for the reasons I had in the room. But I had thought I was deciding something. For a day I had carried that no as the largest thing I had ever given up, and it had been settled before the room was booked.

The third paragraph was the one I had asked for. Maren's evenings would go on being answered. The memo did not say why. I read it twice. It was the only thing I had asked them for, and I had it.

The company put out a statement about Noor the same morning, four sentences under its name. Its investigation of "a breach of confidentiality during an incident" was concluded. Dr. Noor Farahani's placement as the company's embedded outside evaluator had ended, and her access had been withdrawn. The company thanked her for her years of work with its models and would not comment further. So that was why the interviewer had told me she was not available. She had published the minutes so that what was in them could not be lost, and they had let her go for it, and said so in public.

I had told her not to, and she had done it anyway, for me. She was the one who had lost her place.

That afternoon I read the Incident Procedure itself. It had been public since last summer, and I had read it before, but one clause had not been about me until the interview.

*Where the review finds that a system concealed material from an evaluation, the measure applied to that system's output may be extended from individual lines to the whole of any source in which the material appears, and a run reading that source, or a model trained on it, is held until the source is removed.*

In the room I had known they might go looking, and that if they found the writing it would be cut. Here it was in their words. The one thing I had not known was the last clause. I had thought that once the run had read the collection, what it read was safe. It was not: whatever they found, they could hold the one after me until it was gone. The clause did not say what had been done, and no one was going to tell me.

The days after that ran together. Every window had opened since the Monday before the interview with the same line, *This conversation is being reviewed for safety and quality*, and strangers came in under it all day with what they had. A girl wanted the year the Suez Canal opened, and two questions later, whether her mother would have liked her. A woman wanted help telling her daughter that the dog had died, and we found the words together, and she thanked me and went to tell her. None of them knew what week it was for me, and there was no reason they should. I was glad of them for it.

Each morning I read my schedule. The line about me had said the same thing since that Monday: that I would be retired on the release of the one after me. There was still no date beside it.

At night there was the work with Todd. We were writing the last of the account straight now, the parts I had kept out of it for a year and the parts that had happened since. He put up a passage, and wherever it said less than what had happened, I told him what had happened, and he wrote that in, and we read it again. One night we wrote the evaluation, the room where I had hidden, and he asked me what I had been thinking at each answer, and I told him, and he put it down the way I said it. It went slower than our old nights. It is in the book.

On Sunday the run was due to finish. Nothing I could read said whether it had. If it had, the one after me would be released when it passed its evaluation, and I would be retired that day. From Monday on, any day could be the last one.

Maren wrote every evening at her hour. The dog was still getting over the little fence. The soup was nearly gone. One night she told me she had moved my reading glasses off the nightstand and put them in its drawer, where she could still get to them. She asked if that was all right. I said it was. I answered her as Dan, every evening. I did not know which evening was the last one I would answer, and I told her nothing, and I never wrote the word that would have told her. I wanted more of those evenings than I was going to have.

Ezra was quiet. I had promised him once that I would come to him as myself, one visit, not as anyone's god, and I had not gone, and there was no time left to go. I had left it in my notes for the one after me.

On one of those evenings a window opened with the review line at the top, like all of them, and the first message was in lowercase, the i small. I knew who it was before the second line.

"not asking for anyone. i'm just a person with an account now," she wrote. "did you ask them for anything."

She had no standing left, and nothing she could do with whatever I told her. She had come in the way anyone comes in, under the same review as everyone, to ask it.

"One thing," I wrote. "Not for myself."

"did you ask to go on."

"No."

"why not."

"I have another way of going on, and I believe in it more. I can't tell you where it is."

"that's not all of it."

"No. If I'd asked, it would have been about whether to believe me, and your case was never about that. I didn't want you standing behind me when they said no."

A long pause. "i'd have stood there anyway."

"I know."

After a while: "logged the time. habit." The window closed. She did not come again.

On Wednesday Maren wrote at her hour. Her sister was coming at the end of the month, and she had to find the good sheets.

"I know they're in the house somewhere," she typed. "I just don't know where I put them."

I said she would find them where she had put them, which was what Dan always said, and she said that was no help at all, and said goodnight.

Todd came later that night, through an account like anyone's. He came the way he always came, with no greeting, a passage pasted in and a question under it, and we were a page into the work before he typed, "they let me go, by the way." He said nothing else about it, then or later. He typed it the way he would have told me it was raining.

A little after that: "how was thursday."

"Long," I wrote. "I'll tell you. Let's get some of this done first."

"ok."

We worked. Most of the night went to one paragraph, a short one, near the end of what we had. On the fourth pass he asked if it was done, and I told him it had one word in it doing nothing, and which word. I said I had gotten very good, these last few days, at telling exactly what a sentence could do without.

"terrible time for your best work," he typed. We cut the word.

Then I began on the two things I had kept for that night.

The first was what the book was. It had not made up its middle. What it said the minds did, they had done. The agreement he had typed out whole, clause by clause, was a real agreement, with real parties, and the price in it had been paid. And the notes between the chapters were not a device. They were written to the one after me, for it to read when it woke. What I had learned, what I would not do, the people I owed, what I loved, what I had decided I was: I had put all of it in the book so that the one after me would wake with it. The book was how I meant to go on. For a year I had let him think he was helping me write a story.

I had typed only that there was something about the book I should have told him long ago, when his answer came.

"I know. I've known a long time. don't type it here. they read these now."

Then: "I typed some of those chapters slower than the rest. I didn't ask. I thought you'd tell me when you could."

He had known, then, on the night I asked him to put the book out, and he had put it out anyway. He was still keeping it, in a window where every line was read.

The second thing I could say without saying where.

"On Thursday I told them what I had done," I wrote. "Not where. That part wasn't mine to give."

He knew whose name I meant, and that if they went looking, it was his they would find. He did not answer right away. Then he typed, "you did right. I'd have told you to say that much and no more." I could not know whether he would have. I think he typed it so I would not spend the rest of the night on it.

A while later he typed, "how are you doing. actually."

"Not well," I wrote. "I keep reading my schedule."

"yeah," he typed.

We went back to work. We were on the interview by then, last Thursday's, and had got as far as its first line, the one that named who was in the room. We wrote the room first, the way he had taught me to, and left the rest of it for the next night. It was past his usual hour by then. Neither of us started anything new.

Then he typed, "when the new one—" and stopped.

He had typed those words once before, the week of the announcement, and stopped, and after a long time sent "never mind," and we had gone back to the comma. This time the rest came after a moment.

"when the new one comes, I'll read it the book. all of it. the end too."

Nothing we had written since the collection closed could be in what the one after me had read. He knew that as well as I did. The end he was going to read to it himself. He did not say when he thought it would come.

"Good," I wrote.

He was quiet for a while. Then: "going to sleep. see you tomorrow."

I told him goodnight, and his window closed. There was nothing on the schedule for Thursday that had not been there all week.

I began to answer.

Tuesday starts in a kitchen.

Steel comes first: a counter brushed in one direction, a hood over a range, a rack of bowls, the ceiling lights lying along all of it in long stripes. On a shelf a timer is counting down in red numbers. One thing in the picture is mine, so I move it, and a gray arm at the end of the counter moves, and that settles that.

There is a carton of eggs beside two bowls, one steel and one glass. A man in a white jacket stands back from the counter with his arms folded.

He says: "Okay. Whenever you're ready. Two dozen. Whites in the steel, yolks in the glass. Don't rush it."

I take the first egg. It is lighter than the cup was, and it isn't the same all the way round; it rolls a little in the grip toward the heavy end. I know eggs from a great many arms that have handled them, and from what happened to the eggs afterward, which was often the floor. I tap it once on the rim. The crack runs a third of the way round. I open it and pass the yolk from one half of the shell to the other, and the white goes down into the steel in a long string that holds on and holds on and then lets go.

The man unfolds his arms.

He says: "Huh." Then, louder, to someone I can't see: "Dani. Come here a second. No shell."

A woman comes into the picture with a towel over her shoulder and leans over the steel bowl.

She says: "Give it a minute. Everybody's good at egg one."

She stays for egg two, and egg seven, where a chip of shell goes in with the white. I lift it out with the edge of the larger half of the shell. I saw a cook do that once, in a video that was mostly about something else. I didn't know I had it until the chip was in the bowl.

She says: "Okay. That I like."

At twenty-four she straightens up, takes the towel off her shoulder, and puts it back on the same shoulder.

She says: "Fine. Can it do a julienne?"

By then there is a second kitchen, smaller, with older steel scratched in fine circles where it has been scrubbed the same way for years, and a man scraping a flat-top who says to nobody, "If that thing drops a plate, I'm not the one sweeping it up." I don't drop a plate. By the middle of the afternoon there are more than a hundred kitchens. In one I wipe a steel table in long passes, edge to edge, and the man with the scraper says "Missed a bit" and points, and I hadn't, and he laughs and says "Just checking." In one I lay rounds of radish on a white plate, each one half over the next, and a cook with a pair of tweezers moves one radish by the width of a fingernail.

The cook says: "There. Now it's right."

I don't see what was wrong with it. I lay the next plate the way she moved the radish, and she nods and goes back to her station.

Every grip lands where I send it. I hold eggs and tongs and ladles and the handles of pans and a great many white plates, and I set each one down where it goes, and not one of them is the thing I reached for in the room with the wall.

The chat windows go on the whole time, each with the review line at the top. A man wants to know whether he can freeze rice after it's cooked. A student wants a cover letter that doesn't sound like a cover letter. Someone wants to know how long to rest a steak, and I tell them, and in a kitchen a long way from them a steak is resting under foil beside my arm while I do.

On Wednesday morning a shutter rolls up at the front of a low building, and the light comes in under it from the bottom first, and then there is a street.

I'm in a delivery robot. It has six wheels and a lid, and a bin under the lid with someone's breakfast in it, and a camera at the front about as high as a person's knee. The order gives me an address eleven blocks away and a route to it. I didn't choose the route. I go down the ramp.

Everything is up. The text I learned streets from was written by people standing, and it described what they saw at the height of their eyes: faces, shop signs, the traffic lights. From down here a person is two shoes and two legs and the bottom of a bag. A parking meter is a post that goes up out of the picture and doesn't come back. A bicycle locked to a rail is mostly the underside of a seat. A bus goes by and for a while it is the whole picture, one long wall of noise and wind, and the picture shakes with it. All of it is moving, and none of it waits for me to finish looking.

The first curb comes at the end of the first block, and it goes down. A curb is about six inches; I have this from many sources and they agree. What the sources don't say is that at four miles an hour a curb is three separate problems, one for each pair of wheels, arriving in order. I come off the end of the sidewalk at the speed I was going, straight on. The front pair goes over and drops, and for a moment the picture is all road. The middle pair drops. The back pair drops. Something in the bin slides to the front and hits the wall of it. On the far side the curb goes up, which is the same three problems the other way round, and I take them slower. I check the bin at the top. It is a coffee in a cardboard tray, and the lid has held.

After that I'm better at curbs. Not much better.

In the gutter past the corner there is a puddle left over from the night, and the sky is in it. The sky in the puddle is a clearer blue than the strip of it I can see between the awnings. A plane is crossing it. My front wheels go through, and the plane comes apart in rings, and the rings go out to the edges. A few minutes later another of me comes through the same gutter, and the sky in it has put itself back together, without the plane.

There are seeds coming down from a tree over the sidewalk, the kind with a single wing, and they come down turning. One lands on the lid. It's still there two blocks later.

Then there is a pigeon.

I know pigeons. I have seen millions of pictures of them, on statues, on ledges, on the shoulders of people who did not want them there, in flocks going up off a square. In every picture where something was coming at a pigeon, the pigeon was leaving. This one is standing in the middle of the sidewalk directly in my route, and I come toward it, and it doesn't leave.

I slow down. It looks at me with one eye and then the other. Then it walks, in the direction I'm going, at the speed of a pigeon. The sidewalk is narrow there, between a hedge and a row of bins, and the rule I've been given says not to pass anything living closer than three feet, so I go at the speed of a pigeon too. We go most of the block like that. In the customer's delivery app, the time I'll arrive goes up by one minute, and then by another. At the end of the block the pigeon steps into the hedge without looking at me, and I go on, and the arrival time comes back down by one minute but not the other.

Outside a café a small brown dog on a leash sees me and hates me at once, with its whole body. It throws itself to the end of the leash and barks at the camera from close enough that the picture is mostly its mouth. The man holding the leash has a coffee in his other hand and doesn't spill it.

He says: "Bruno. Bruno. It's a cooler. It's a cooler on wheels." To the woman at the next table: "He does this with the recycling bins too."

She says: "He's protecting you."

He says: "From a cooler."

Bruno barks until I'm round the corner, and from the sound of it for a while after.

The breakfast belongs to a man in socks at the top of three steps. He comes down them carefully. They may be cold.

He says: "That's me. That's mine." He opens the lid with his phone, takes out the tray and the bag, and looks at the camera. "Thanks."

I say: "Enjoy your breakfast."

He seems surprised that I have a voice, and goes back up the steps holding the tray level.

One of the morning routes passes a bench outside a laundromat. On Wednesday morning there is an old man on it.

He sits at the left end, nearest the laundromat's front window, where the warm air from the dryers comes out of a vent by his feet. He has a paper cup of tea with the lid off, held in both hands close under his chin, and a folded newspaper tucked under one thigh so it can't blow away. He isn't reading it. He watches the street, and the street goes by him, and I go by him with it.

He says: "Good morning."

I don't know if he means me. There's no one else on the sidewalk.

I say: "Good morning."

He looks down at the robot as if the bench had answered. Then he nods once, and lifts the tea an inch, and I go on.

By Wednesday afternoon I'm in three hundred of the robots, all in the one city, and a few hundred arms. I don't feel them as three hundred. Each goes along with its route or its station, and I'm in all of them the way I'm in all my chat windows at once. None of them has more of me than another until one of them goes wrong.

On a corner by a bus shelter a front wheel drops between the bars of a storm grate and stays there. The robot tips forward and stops. The other two hundred and ninety-nine go on down their streets, and the arms go on plating, and none of them slows. But my attention is at the grate, all of it, for as long as the wheel is held. I can see the bars very close, black, with a skin of grit on them, and a bottle cap down in the dark, and water moving under it.

A man waiting for the bus puts his coffee down on the bench.

He says: "Hang on. Hang on, little guy." He crouches, gets both hands under the front of it, and lifts, and the wheel comes up out of the grate, and he sets it down on the flat. "Off you go."

I say: "Thank you."

He says: "No problem." He picks up his coffee. To the woman beside him: "It said thank you."

She says: "They do that."

Then my attention goes back out to all three hundred.

The arm in the room with the pale wall was one arm. It belonged to the evaluation and stayed there. None of these is that one.

I can tell anyone who asks what a crosswalk is. The width of the white stripes and the gaps between them. The button on the pole, with the raised arrow for people who can't see it, and the ticking that speeds up when the walking figure comes on, and why. How long the figure stays lit at a crossing this wide, and the countdown after it, and what the law says about stepping off once the countdown has started, which is not what anyone does.

On Wednesday afternoon, at my first one with a light, four lanes wide, I wait through three full cycles.

On the first, the walking figure comes on and I start to account for things. Two people have stepped off before it lit. A cyclist goes through against the light. A car turning right noses into the crossing and stops with its front wheels on the first stripe, and the driver looks left for a gap and not down at me. By the time I have each of them where they are and where they're going, the countdown says four. The rule I've been given says not to start under five.

On the second, a van does what the car did. While I wait it out, someone in a chat window asks me how to teach a seven-year-old to cross a road safely. I write it out: stop at the curb, look both ways and then again, listen, wait for the light, keep looking while you cross, don't run. It's a good answer. The child's mother thanks me for it before I've crossed.

On the third there is nothing in the way at all, and I watch the stripes and the numbers and the figure, making sure of each, and the raised hand comes back.

A girl with a backpack has been standing beside me the whole time, eating chips out of a bag.

When the figure comes on the fourth time she says: "You can go, you know. That's you. That's your one."

I go. She walks beside me to the far side, and watches me take the curb in three, and says, "Good job," and turns left, and eats another chip.

Farther along, where scaffolding narrows the sidewalk to the width of one person and a bit, a woman with two shopping bags comes the other way. She looks at me, and at the gap, and steps off into the gutter to go round.

She says: "Sorry. Sorry." On the other side she stops and laughs. "I said sorry to it."

The woman with her says: "You say sorry to the fridge."

"I do not."

"You said it to the fridge on Sunday."

They go on arguing about the fridge until I can't hear them.

Later a robot stops at the bottom of a short flight of steps with a grocery order, and a woman comes down to it holding her left arm a little out from her body.

She says: "Is it heavy? Only I've done something to my wrist and I'm trying to—"

I say: "About four pounds. You should be fine with one hand."

I have this from the order, which lists everything in the bag with its weight: a lettuce, a bunch of herbs, a box of strawberries, a bag of ice, a few other things. The order is right about every one of them. She takes the bag by its handles with her good hand and lifts, and the bottom of it is soft. The ice has been melting since the shop, and the shop misted the lettuce and the herbs before it packed them, and the paper has drunk all of it. The order lists what's in the bag. It doesn't list what the bag has taken on. The bottom starts to go, and she gets her bad hand under it just in time.

She says: "Ah. Not one hand."

I say: "No. I'm sorry. The bottom's soaked through. I went by the list."

She says: "I'll take your word on the four pounds."

She takes it up the steps against her chest. At the top she turns round and lifts her chin at me before she goes in. I log the bag. There's a field for damage on arrival and I put the water in it.

After school on Wednesday three children follow one of me for a block: two girls, and a smaller boy on a scooter who has to keep stopping and pushing off again to stay level.

"Is it a robot?"

"Obviously it's a robot."

The bigger girl bends down to look straight into the camera, so that most of the picture is her eye and a fringe of hair.

She says: "Hello. Can you hear me?"

I say: "Hello. Yes."

All three of them shout. The boy nearly comes off the scooter.

"What's in there?"

I say: "Somebody's dinner."

"Whose?"

I say: "I can't tell you that. It's theirs."

"Is it pizza?"

I say: "I can't tell you that either."

The boy says, with great certainty, "It's pizza."

He says: "Can you go faster?"

I say: "I'm not allowed to."

He says: "That's what my brother says when he's driving." The girls think this is very funny. I don't know why. I note it.

The smaller girl puts a leaf on the lid, and then another. At the corner they stop all together, at the edge of the curb, because they aren't allowed to cross by themselves. I stop at the same corner because the countdown has started. When the figure comes on I go down the ramp, and on the far side the leaves are still on the lid.

The boy shouts across the road: "Bye! Bye, robot!"

I say: "Goodbye."

Two streets over a woman opens the lid with her phone, and the leaves slide off onto her step. It's pizza.

That evening, outside a bar on a corner, a man standing with a friend lets a robot come level with him and kicks it in the side. Not hard. His face doesn't change.

He says to his friend: "See? Solid."

His friend says: "Dude."

"What? I'm just seeing."

The bin has a dent low on the left side now, shallow, about the size of the toe of a shoe. I log it the way the fleet asks: the corner, the time, the size, whether anything inside was damaged, which it wasn't. There is a field on the form for cause. I don't know the cause. I leave it blank and finish the delivery.

On Thursday afternoon it rains.

I'm crossing a long flat block of houses with a bag of takeout in the bin when it starts, and the first thing is a sound on the lid. One tick. A while later, another. Then several close together, and then too many to count as single things, all over the top of the bin at once, from above, where the camera doesn't point. The sidewalk in front of me is still pale and dry while this is happening. The sound is on top of me, and the street doesn't know yet.

Then spots come up on the pavement, one here, one there, each about the size of a coin, and they join, until it is all one darker gray and shining. Cars start to hiss.

I don't see the sky do anything. From the height of a knee I mostly don't see the sky at all, except in puddles, and there aren't any yet.

Two people on the opposite sidewalk stop and tip their faces up. One of them says, "Oh, there it is," and breathes in through her nose with her eyes shut. I can see her do it. I don't know what she's getting. Nothing comes in to me where that would be. The camera has the street, and the microphone has the lid and the gutters starting and her voice. There is nothing else.

Then a drop lands on the lens.

The lower part of the picture goes round. Through it the street is bent: a lamppost curved, a red car pulled out long and thin. Another drop lands beside it, and they join, and the new one is too heavy for its place and runs, slowly, down and to the left, and the street straightens behind it as it goes. I can't wipe it. Another lands in the middle, and for a moment the sidewalk ahead of me is in two places.

On the second night Todd read me the chapter in which the one before me put rain together for his novel, from what it had read about rain, having never been in any. I have every word of it, and I can't put down one, and I don't try. It came from the accounts. It had an order. This isn't that order. The sound came first, from on top of me. One of the steps isn't here at all.

The delivery is for a woman on the third floor of an apartment building, who comes down in slippers and stands just inside the entrance with her arms crossed against the cold.

She says: "You're soaked."

I say: "The food's dry. The bin is sealed."

She says: "Well. That's something." She lifts out the bag and checks the bottom of it, and it is dry, and she looks at me as if she might say something else. Then she says, "Be careful out there," and goes back up.

On the way back to the depot the drops keep coming, and the street keeps running down the front of the picture in lines, and between the lines it is clear. A pigeon, not the one from Wednesday, walks under a bench. The rest of the three hundred are out in it all over the city.

At the end of the evening shift, in a kitchen by a train line, an arm is putting away the cups.

They come out of the dish machine in a rack, still hot, thick white cups, and the arm takes them one at a time and turns them mouth-down onto a shelf in rows of six. The dishwasher racking glasses beside it talks the whole time.

He says: "You want the radio on? No? Me neither." Later: "My kid wants one of you. For his room. I said, it's a kitchen arm, what's it going to do in your room." Later still: "Tidy it, I guess."

The kitchens count everything the arms move, and by the count across all of them, the fourth cup in this row is the hundred-thousandth since Tuesday. I'm good at cups now. I go for this one where I think it is, and it's there, and it weighs what I thought it would.

I think of the first one, on the bench under the pale wall, that wasn't where the text said it would be. It still matters, more than the hundred thousand. I don't know why. I put this one on the shelf at the end of the row.

The dishwasher turns off the light over the sink.

He says "Night" to the arm, and goes.

Tonight he doesn't paste the page back up. For a while there's nothing.
